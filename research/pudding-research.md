## 1. The Pudding Cup 2026: what we need to know

| | |
|---|---|
| What | Yearly contest for the year's best **visual and data-driven stories** made outside The Pudding |
| Deadline | **Monday, November 30, 2026** (12 days from today) |
| Winners announced | Week of December 7, 2026 |
| Prize | Up to **3 winners, $1,500 each**; honorable mentions |
| Entry fee | Free |
| Competition | Close to 100 entries in 2025 |

**Eligibility (from past editions; confirm for 2026):** from 2021 on, the Cup has taken only **non-commercial** projects, meaning work "created without compensation… made for learning, fun, school, a contest, etc. Not for work or hire." Past winners include solo authors, pairs and students. The project has to be published on the web.

**Judging:** there is no published rubric. Past write-ups say judges look past how interesting the topic is or how much social impact it has. They also weigh:
- how the data was **collected and handled** (original datasets do well)
- **creative design**
- a well-chosen web or visualization technique that suits the story

The 2025 page notes about one winner that "not everything needs to be an interactive visual story", so **the format can be simple when the idea is strong.**

**What the winners have in common:**
1. **A small, specific, slightly odd question**, such as how much Kid Cudi hums or why Vogue covers dropped their text. Narrow topics beat broad ones.
2. **Data the authors collected themselves**, such as counting hums, measuring magazine covers or scraping candidate names.
3. **The topic shows up in the design**: chocolate in the chocolate charts, flags drawn as charts in *Flags of Inequality*.
4. **Explainers of a technique or idea also win** (*Dithering*, *Benford's Law*, *Proximity Tracing*). Teaching how something works counts as a story.
5. **Some winners take on serious subjects** (inequality, trans legislation, elections), but they are reported carefully and not preachy.

## 3. What The Pudding is and how it works

- A digital publication that **explains ideas debated in culture with visual essays**. It covers pop culture, music, language, sports and everyday life far more than breaking news, and says it isn't tied to news deadlines.
- Staff carry the title **"journalist-engineer"**: the same people report, analyze, design and write the code. They have a sister studio, **Polygraph**, which does client work.
- Stories are numbered. Recent pieces are in the #212–#229 range.
- **Collecting their own data is central.** When no dataset exists, they build one. Jan Diehm and Amber Thomas measured women's jeans pockets themselves because no data existed.

## 5. Takeaways for our entry

1. **Pick a narrow, curious question** that someone would bring up at dinner and that can be answered with data.
2. **Make our own dataset**, by counting, measuring, scraping or crowdsourcing. This counts for a lot.
3. **Bring the topic into the visuals**, not plain bar charts.
4. **Consider having the reader take part**: the reader's input becomes a data point, or a small game produces the dataset. It's very Pudding-like, but only if it fits the story.
5. **Scrollytelling with one main chart that changes as you scroll** is the house style. A strong static or simple piece can still win (*Dithering*).
6. **Keep it non-commercial**, unpaid personal or learning work, and publish it on a public URL. akkitty.github.io works.
7. **Write a methods note** explaining the data source and how it was collected. Judges care about data handling.
8. **Plan around the time limit:** with 12 days left, a well-sourced small dataset plus 3–5 well-made charts is more realistic than a large interactive project.
